# Market research: how strong ads get small creators to click (v2)

Only facts in `product-brief.md` are used for the "apply to this offer" lines.

## 1. What I could and could not access

- Web search worked. I got summaries of pages from Google, TikTok, Meta and practitioner blogs.
- Opening pages failed. Every fetch (support.google.com, ads.tiktok.com, airbridge.io, socialmediaexaminer.com, searchengineland.com) was blocked by the network. So I read search summaries only, not full pages.

Evidence labels: "sourced" = a search result summarised it (URL given, page not opened by me). "general practice (not verified here)" = common craft knowledge, no source.

## 2. Ten patterns

**1. Qualifier hook (call out the viewer)**
- First 2 s: big text naming who it is for, spoken or shown at once.
- Format: text-on-video, with a voice or a talking creator.
- Pacing: 15 s or less; 1 to 2 cuts in the first 3 s.
- Offer and CTA: the qualifier comes first, the offer second, CTA last.
- Sound: voice plus light music. Captions mirror the words.
- Why it works: the right viewer stops, the wrong one scrolls. Sources say a good hook names the person.
- This offer: open on a big "1,000 to 50,000 subscribers" with "Free to apply."
- Evidence: sourced (hook should name the person, open a loop, show the product by second 3). https://motionapp.com/library/talk/savannah-sanchez-shares-her-top-43-ad-hooks-for-apps-and-services/ (via search summary, single vendor view, not controlled research).

**2. Problem-first relatable hook**
- First 2 s: say out loud a complaint the viewer already thinks.
- Format: talking creator, direct to camera.
- Pacing: 15 to 30 s; hook, problem, fix, CTA.
- Offer and CTA: the fix appears after the problem. CTA is short and last.
- Sound: spoken voice, casual tone.
- Why it works: it feels relevant before any pitch.
- This offer: the brief's "no pitching, no cold emails" is the problem. "You do not look for sponsors yourself" is the fix.
- Evidence: sourced. https://www.rocketshiphq.com/how-to-create-ugc-ads-mobile-apps/ and https://www.gotolstoy.com/blog/how-to-structure-ugc-ads (search summary).

**3. Direct-to-camera, casual, simple production**
- First 2 s: a person already mid-sentence, tight framing.
- Format: talking creator, phone-style.
- Pacing: short; cuts on sentence breaks.
- Offer and CTA: said plainly in speech, repeated as text at the end.
- Sound: rich voice audio.
- Why it works: Google's guidance says casual, conversational, native-feeling ads suit Shorts. A formal tone is a listed "don't".
- This offer: a generated presenter says "Free to apply. No deal, no commission." Never as a customer, never a result (brief section 9).
- Evidence: sourced. https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16041697 and https://business.google.com/us/think/search-and-video/short-form-video-advertising-tips/ (search summaries).

**4. Native-feeling, not ad-looking**
- First 2 s: looks like normal feed content, not a studio ad.
- Format: POV or creator-style clip.
- Pacing: matches organic Shorts and Reels.
- Offer and CTA: the offer is spoken or in text, not a logo slate.
- Sound: voice or trending-style audio.
- Why it works: Google says ads that blend with organic content do better. Practitioners say creator-style beats polished studio for app installs (a blog claim with no data cited).
- This offer: a bright, messy filming corner with a phone on a stand (brief section 8). Generated, no readable screens.
- Evidence: sourced (Google part). https://business.google.com/us/think/search-and-video/short-form-video-advertising-tips/. The "beats studio" part is only a blog claim: https://megadigital.ai/en/blog/tiktok-ad-creatives.

**5. Text-on-screen carrier (works muted)**
- First 2 s: one short line of text carries the whole hook.
- Format: text-on-video over simple b-roll.
- Pacing: 3 to 7 words per screen, one idea each; cuts every 1 to 3 s.
- Offer and CTA: offer as text card; CTA as a text card or simple animation at the end.
- Sound: music or voice under it.
- Why it works: many people watch muted, so the message must read without sound.
- This offer: three text cards: "Is your channel too small?" / "Free to apply." / "Apply."
- Evidence: sourced. https://www.funnel.io/blog/instagram-ads-best-practices-high-conversion and https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16041697 (text overlays plus sound).

**6. Fast cut b-roll with voice-over**
- First 2 s: a strong visual, with the first spoken word inside 1.5 s.
- Format: skit-lite or b-roll montage.
- Pacing: roughly 0.5 to 1.0 s per shot in the hook; about 10 to 15 cuts per 10 s at the fastest. One blog gives this, not a benchmark.
- Offer and CTA: offer by second 3 to 5; CTA at the end.
- Sound: voice-over plus music. Meta is reported to say music plus voiceover lifts reaction (about 15%, second-hand).
- Why it works: motion holds the eye while the voice explains.
- This offer: quick shots of a thumb ticking yes and a hand pushing a sponsor away ("you can turn down any sponsor").
- Evidence: sourced (pacing, weak). https://ecommercefastlane.com/?p=178649 and https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-ads-trends/489813/ (search summaries).

**7. Soft, low-pressure CTA**
- First 2 s: not applicable; this is about the ending and the middle.
- Format: any of the above.
- Pacing: the ask comes after value is shown.
- Offer and CTA: no hard sell. Say what the viewer gets, then one plain action. One source tested delaying the app name.
- Sound: calm voice.
- Why it works: for a free sign-up there is little to fear, so a calm ask feels honest.
- This offer: "No deal, no commission. Apply." One CTA only.
- Evidence: sourced, weak (one vendor's own test). https://www.airbridge.io/en/resources/handbook/ugc/04-hooks-and-soft-cta (search summary).

**8. Benefit first, CTA seen more than once**
- First 2 s: the most important benefit to the viewer.
- Format: any short vertical format.
- Pacing: 15 s or less where possible.
- Offer and CTA: show the CTA more than once, anchor it at the end.
- Sound: voice plus music.
- Why it works: Google guidance leads with the benefit that matters most and repeats the CTA. TikTok's playbooks say the value proposition should land inside the first 3 to 6 s.
- This offer: lead with "A sponsor chosen for your small channel", then end on "Apply."
- Evidence: sourced. https://blog.google/products/ads-commerce/youtube-shorts-ads-select-lineups-abcds/ and https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/Creative_Codes_ENG.pdf (search summaries). The "90% of recall in 6 s" figure is a TikTok claim I could not read.

**9. Number as the visual packshot**
- First 2 s: one large number or "0" fills the frame.
- Format: text-on-video, motion graphic.
- Pacing: 1 to 2 held shots, short.
- Offer and CTA: the number is the offer. CTA appears on a clean end card.
- Sound: a clear beat or sting, then voice.
- Why it works: a service has no product shot, so a number gives the eye something concrete.
- This offer: "1,000 to 50,000" large, then a price tag reading 0.
- Evidence: general practice (not verified here). It follows the brief's own section 8.

**10. Short test-many-hooks structure**
- First 2 s: varies; only the hook changes between versions.
- Format: same middle and CTA, 3 to 5 hook variants.
- Pacing: 15 to 30 s.
- Offer and CTA: identical across variants, so results compare fairly.
- Sound: same track across variants.
- Why it works: sources advise testing many cheap hook angles, then keeping the few that perform.
- This offer: one body, hooks from patterns 1, 2, 5 and 9.
- Evidence: sourced. https://www.rocketshiphq.com/how-to-create-ugc-ads-mobile-apps/ (search summary).

Count: 9 sourced (some only through weak or second-hand sources), 1 general practice.

## 3. Five things that look bland or fake, and what strong ads do

All five rows are general practice (not verified here), except where noted.

1. Stock-looking footage and a logo slate. Strong ads look like a real person's feed content. (Native-feel point is sourced under pattern 4.)
2. Vague promise ("grow your channel"). Strong ads make one specific promise a viewer can repeat back.
3. Talking about the company. Strong ads talk about what the viewer gets.
4. Formal tone and long intro. Strong ads use a casual voice and start on the point. (Formal tone is a Google "don't": https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16041697.)
5. Fake proof: screenshots, invented reviews, "as seen" claims. Strong ads show the offer and the control the viewer keeps. See section 5.

## 4. Platform notes

All from search summaries, not from pages I opened. Verify against Meta, TikTok and Google pages before final export.

- Reels safe zone: one agency guide gives 14% top, 35% bottom, 6% each side, attributed to Meta's Ads Guide. Source: https://coinis.com/how-to/design-facebook-reels-ad (second-hand). Ads Manager has a safe zone overlay, per the same summaries. Meta's own text not verified.
- Reels length: 60 s or less, 9:16; Ads Manager recommends 15 s or less. Source: https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/ads-on-reels-updated-options-for-marketers/ (second-hand).
- Reels sound and captions: plan for sound-off, but include voice or music. Auto-captions are available at upload. Captions and CTA buttons can be cut off outside the safe zone. Sources: https://quickframe.mountain.com/?p=15562 and https://websitebuilderexpert.com/news/meta-updates-reels-ads-guide/ (second-hand).
- Meta claims "2x delivery, 34.5% lower cost per result" for vertical, sound, safe-zone Reels. Only blogs repeat it. Treat as unverified.
- Shorts format and length: 9:16 recommended. Up to 3 minutes allowed, but only the first 60 s play in the Shorts feed. Source: https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/16041697.
- Shorts sound: Google says sound (music, voice or both) is shown to raise conversions by over 20%. Same source. Text overlays are recommended alongside sound.
- Shorts CTA button: appears at 3 s for Performance Max, App and Demand Gen, and at 10 s for Video View and Reach. Same source.
- Meta feed 1:1 and 16:9, feed caption rules, and Shorts safe-zone margins: unverified. Not found in any result.

## 5. Honesty: three "do not do" notes

1. No fake income screenshots. No money amounts of any kind (brief section 9). Meta restricts income and outcome claims, per secondary guides and its "unrealistic outcomes" policy page: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/deceptive-content/unrealistic-outcomes (title seen in search, text not read). The FTC treats earnings claims as an enforcement focus (secondary analysis, not read first-hand).
2. No fake testimonials. No invented customer, review, rating or "it worked for me" line. A generated person talks about the offer only, never as a customer. General practice (not verified here); also the brief's hard rule.
3. No fake urgency. No countdowns, "last places", deadlines or limited spots. None exist (brief section 7). Never imply a sponsor is guaranteed: applying does not promise one. I found no source on timer ads specifically, so this is general practice (not verified here) plus the brief's rule.
